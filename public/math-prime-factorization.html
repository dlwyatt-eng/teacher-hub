Prime factorization

Break a number into prime factors. Explain why you are finished.

Paper and pencil are enough. No counters or special printing needed.

What counts as finished?

A prime number has exactly two positive factors: 1 and itself. A composite number has more than two. The number 1 is neither.

Discuss: which of 2, 4, 7 and 9 can be split into two whole-number factors greater than 1?

Prime-factorize 42.

  1. Show a factor tree or a clear sequence of factor splits.
  2. Write 42 as a product of prime numbers.
  3. Explain how you know you are finished.

Teacher script, timing and answers · keep closed when projecting

50-minute guide: 5 minutes prime/composite review; about 10 minutes for the video with pauses (adjust as needed); 10 minutes comparing both trees for 24; 15 minutes practice and reasoning; 5 minutes discussion; 5 minutes independent exit check. Carry the 60 challenge forward if time is short.

Say: “Every split must multiply back to its parent. A prime end number is finished. A composite end number must split again. We record only the prime end numbers, including repeats.” Circle prime ends; never introduce a branch of 1 and the same number.

Answers: 2 and 7 are prime; 4 = 2 × 2 and 9 = 3 × 3. 18 = 2 × 3 × 3; 30 = 2 × 3 × 5; 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3; 60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5; 42 = 2 × 3 × 7. For the error, 2 × 3 × 6 equals 36, but 6 is composite. Multiplying internal nodes as well as end numbers counts the same factorization more than once.

Assess: the student preserves each product, reaches only prime ends, retains repeated primes and verifies the result. Support with 12 or 18 and a multiplication chart; ask students ready for more to compare two trees for 72. Exponent notation is optional; expanded products are enough.

Follow-up: Pack It / Sync It: counter lesson applies factors to identical groups and GCF. Use it in a later lesson, after checking today’s understanding. It is not part of Monday’s required prime-factorization work.
