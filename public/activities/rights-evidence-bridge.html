Teach the bridge, not another project

Purpose: move from Juniper Park's rule, evidence and check-back thinking into the existing Two Countries Shared Questions inquiry. This can also stand alone. Use it in an available block; no lesson date or new deadline is assigned.

Prepare: one screen, pencils and scrap paper, or the optional two-page student PDF. Read the six fictional cards aloud as needed. No student device, new account, personal experience or family disclosure is needed. Do not ask learners to represent a country, disability or hardship.

  1. 5 min: introduce the three rights and distinguish a right from a particular way to support it.
  2. 8 min: read Alder A1–A3. Invite a first claim, then reveal and discuss the model.
  3. 10 min: partners use Bay B1–B3 to make a claim, evidence link and limit. Read aloud or scribe without lowering the thinking demand.
  4. 5 min: each learner repairs the overclaim. Ask what evidence would change their answer.
  5. 7 min: transfer the same questions into a provisional two-country search. Save this thinking in the existing booklet, not a new required upload.

Look for: a right connected to an actual detail, a claim limited to the people/place/time studied, and a useful next question. A different supported interpretation is welcome. Assess the explanation, not the number of boxes completed.

Teaching notes and sample reasoning

Bay B2 supports a narrow finding: the observed route arrived before lessons on five days. It cannot establish that everyone in Bay could reach school. B3 adds a direct experience and a missing group; it does not invalidate B2. A2 and B2 use the same five-day window and one route each, but we do not know how those routes were selected or how many pupils depend on them. Do not calculate a national access rate or rank whole places.

Reasonable follow-ups include asking about the outer-settlement routes, checking longer-term records, or listening to pupils not on the council. An improvement might be a trial route designed with those pupils, followed by arrival records and accessible feedback. It is a proposal, not a guaranteed solution. Article 12 calls for taking children's views seriously, not automatically accepting every requested option. Rights do not depend on good behaviour, citizenship or completing responsibilities. This exercise identifies a rights question, not a legal verdict.

Independent check: a defensible repair is “Bay's observed bus reached school before lessons on five days, but B3 leaves access from the outer settlement uncertain.” If a learner repeats “all children,” return to who, where and when in B2. If they only name a right, ask them to connect it to one source detail.

Curriculum connection: evidence and source use, perspectives, ethical judgment, and how decisions affect human rights. This short local model is practice for real international comparison; it does not by itself teach all Grade 6 government content. Official B.C. Grade 6 Social Studies.

1 · Name the right · 5 minutes

The same right. Different barriers.

Education · Article 28

Children are entitled to education. Primary schooling should be free.

Fair treatment · Article 2

Children's rights apply to every child without discrimination.

Being heard · Article 12

Children can express views on decisions affecting them. Adults should take those views seriously.

A right is not a reward

You do not earn rights through good behaviour. Adults and governments have responsibilities to help protect them.

Short teaching paraphrases, not the full Convention. Read UNICEF's children's version. These rights are connected.

Discuss: A school charges no fees. Is that enough to show that every child can attend? Think of a question about transport, accessibility or language support. A bus is one possible support for a right; it is not the right itself.

2 · Read together · 8 minutes

Alder: what does this evidence allow us to say?

Fictional practice: Alder and Bay are invented districts, not real countries. Every card below is teacher-created. The invented October dates help us practise checking time. Do not cite these cards as real-world research.

A1 · What is planned?

Alder district notice

Fictional education office · published 1 October, model year

Primary school has no fees. A school bus is scheduled to leave the outer settlement at 8:10 and arrive before lessons begin at 8:30.

A2 · What happened?

One route's arrival log

Fictional school office · published 12 October; observations 5–9 October, model year

The bus from the outer settlement arrived after 8:30 on three of the five observed days. This log covers one route at one school.

A3 · Whose experience?

Pupil council notes

Fictional pupil council · published 12 October; meeting 9 October, model year

Six pupils who use that bus asked for an earlier departure. The notes record their request but no reply from the transport office.

Name a right. Use a detail. Explain its limit.

Reveal one possible model after discussing

Claim: Children on the observed Alder route faced a barrier to arriving for the start of lessons that week.

Evidence + connection: A1 promises a before-lessons arrival, but A2 records three late arrivals. Reliable transport could help those pupils use their right to education.

Limit: One route over five days cannot describe every child in Alder. A3 shows a request, not whether adults later responded.

Next question: What caused the delays, and what changed after pupils asked? We could seek a later transport-office reply and hear from pupils on other routes.

3 · Try it with a partner · 10 minutes

Bay: compare the same question

Shared question: How do transport decisions help children get to school? These are fictional practice records, not evidence about real countries.

B1 · What is planned?

Bay district notice

Fictional education office · published 1 October, model year

Primary school has no fees. After hearing pupil requests, the transport office is testing an earlier departure for the harbour bus.

B2 · What happened?

One route's arrival log

Fictional school office · published 12 October; observations 5–9 October, model year

The harbour bus arrived before lessons on all five observed days. This log covers one route at one school.

B3 · Whose experience?

Pupil council notes

Fictional pupil council · published 12 October; meeting 9 October, model year

Six harbour-route pupils said the earlier bus helped them. Two pupils from an outer settlement said the trial did not serve their area. The notes give no information about their journey.

  1. Use one Bay card to make a careful claim. Name a right and explain the connection.
  2. Find another card that adds information or shows a limit. Can both records be true?
  3. Compare one Alder and one Bay record using the same question. Say who, where and when the comparison covers.
  4. Suggest one useful next source or person to hear from. Explain why.

“The source shows ___ for ___ during ___. This matters for ___ because ___. It does not tell us ___. Next we need ___.” You may give a supported answer without reaching the same conclusion as another pair.

4 · Your own understanding check · 5 minutes

Repair a claim that goes too far

Claim to check: “Bay protects every child's right to education because its bus was on time.”

  1. Say which part the evidence supports and which part goes beyond it.
  2. Rewrite the claim with a card code, a clear limit, and a question worth investigating.

Challenge if ready: do the different records conflict, or do they describe different people or parts of the issue? Explain without ranking either district as “good” or “bad.”

You may speak, sketch with labels, dictate or write. Keep your own reasoning even if you worked with a partner earlier.

5 · Carry the thinking into your inquiry · 7 minutes

One question. Two countries. More than one story.

  1. Choose provisionally: a question you care about and two countries. You may change them after a first search. Every learner investigates both countries.
  2. Name a rights connection: education, fair treatment, being heard or another right you check in the UNICEF reference. For example, “How do children in different communities get to school?”
  3. Try a search: combine your country, issue and one useful term, such as “school transport” or “children's views.” Keep your own question; these are optional examples.
  4. Ask what each source can do: a policy explains a promise; a dated report or dataset helps check experience; a person's account adds a perspective. One source rarely answers everything.
  5. Record and check: author/organization, title/link, publication date, event/data date, people/place covered, evidence and purpose. Find an independent source that adds or challenges something. Repeated copies of one story are not independent evidence.

Make one source note for each country when research begins. Compare the same issue, similar time periods, and the same definitions. If the sources are not comparable, explain the limit rather than making a ranking. Look for differences within each country too.

Students discover sources with class guidance. Use read-aloud, paired searching or teacher-provided excerpts when helpful. If you cannot find suitable evidence, ask for support or revise the question. Do not contact people or post outside class without the teacher's approval.

Keep the existing inquiry: start or refine page 2; use the news routine on page 9 and evidence work on pages 10–12 when ready. November 20 is a connection, not a deadline. No extra required portfolio post or homework is added.
